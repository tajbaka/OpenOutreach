# Discovery Question Design

Use this reference when preparing, revising, or discussing account-specific call questions. The goal is not to produce a long questionnaire. It is to give the operator a small number of high-leverage conversational doorways, with branches that expose the evidence needed to shape a relevant value proposition and assemble the right buying group.

## Core pattern

For each important topic, write one natural main question. Prepare optional branches across five dimensions:

1. **Evidence or example:** What actually happened? Can the buyer describe a specific opportunity, customer, workflow, or recent event?
2. **Current process:** How is the work handled today? What is repeatable and what is improvised?
3. **Friction and consequence:** Where does it slow down, what creates risk, and what happens when nothing changes?
4. **Ownership and stakeholders:** Who owns the outcome, performs the work, evaluates the solution, supplies evidence, or influences the next step?
5. **Desired outcome:** What would meaningfully improve, and how would the account recognize success?

Do not mechanically ask all five. Ask the main question, listen, then select one or two branches that deepen what the buyer actually said. Prefer requests for a concrete example over a chain of abstract hypotheticals.

## Translate discovery into positioning

Questions should collect information that changes the later pitch. For each main question, note the signals to listen for and the corresponding positioning direction. Examples:

- Repeated customer-specific security reviews may support a reusable-evidence and reduced-duplication value proposition.
- Prime or integrator hesitation may support partner enablement, credibility, and a clearer evaluation path.
- Active agency demand blocked by a requirement may support opportunity-unblocking and procurement-readiness positioning.
- A repeatable hosted service may support a reusable authorization or Marketplace strategy across customers.
- Bespoke, customer-managed, or on-premises deployments may require different packaging or may narrow the value of a Marketplace motion.
- Existing control evidence and a current scoped audit may support building from established operating discipline, without claiming that one framework automatically satisfies another.
- No defined buyer, pipeline, use case, or route to market means authorization should not be presented as a substitute for GTM strategy.

Never make the buyer prove the seller's value proposition. Questions such as “Would Marketplace visibility help?” or “What would make our investment worthwhile?” often push the burden of persuasion onto the buyer. Discover the buyer's motion and friction first, then state the relevant commercial implication and ask whether it matches their experience.

## Recommended topic groups for an early GTM conversation

Choose only the groups relevant to the account and current evidence:

1. Current route to market and source of opportunities.
2. Strongest buyer, mission, and use case.
3. Live demand, sales friction, and consequences.
4. Product packaging, deployment model, and repeatability.
5. Existing trust or security baseline when commercially relevant, such as whether the in-scope product is covered by a current SOC 2 Type I or Type II report.
6. Strategic outcome and timing.
7. People who should shape the next evaluation or working session.

For a normal call, prefer five to eight main questions. Subquestions are operator prompts, not a quota.

## Stakeholder discovery by role

Do not collapse everyone into “the decision-maker.” Determine roles from evidence:

- **Champion:** wants the initiative to succeed and helps navigate the account.
- **Commercial owner:** owns the relevant GTM result, pipeline, partnership, or business outcome.
- **End user:** experiences the operational problem or will use the resulting workflow.
- **Technical evaluator:** assesses architecture, integration, deployment, or product fit.
- **Security or evidence owner:** maintains controls, audits, policies, and supporting evidence.
- **Executive sponsor or economic buyer:** supports the initiative and controls or influences investment.
- **Approver:** must approve security, procurement, finance, or another buying requirement.
- **Authorized signatory:** can legally execute the agreement.

At an introductory call, discover who owns the problem and who should participate in the next evaluation. Natural prompts include:

- “Who owns that work today?”
- “Who feels this problem most directly?”
- “Who has the strongest view on the technical or security side?”
- “Whose concerns would we need to address?”
- “If a focused working session makes sense, who should be in the room?”

Do not pull approval, procurement, budget authority, or legal-signature questions forward before the buyer has experienced enough value and the current sales step requires them. Introduce those roles later according to the canonical sales-motion timing.

## Writing the call block

Order the main questions as the conversation should naturally deepen: broad context, concrete evidence, friction and consequence, relevant operating detail, desired outcome, then next participants and action. Under each main question, include only the most useful conditional branches. Add short “listen for” or “position toward” notes when they materially help the operator respond.

Begin with **Confirm context:** and one concise sentence confirming known account context and the evaluation focus. Bold only the `Confirm context:` prefix; keep the sentence in regular weight. This is spoken after the introductions; do not write the introductions. Immediately follow with the questions. Do not add setup, how-to-use guidance, a separate purpose/recap, meeting metadata, pacing, preparation checklists, or a separate closing script. Put logistics and seller preparation obligations in the tracker’s account-status or task-detail fields.

Use the Xerox-style hierarchy as the default in the single next-call cell:

1. A bold main line containing the number, a short uppercase topic title, an em dash, and the spoken question in quotation marks: `1. FOLLOW A REAL FINDING — “Could you walk us through one recent finding?”`. Bold the whole main line, including the question. Keep the topic label distinct from the question; a numbered question alone omits the scanning cue the user prefers.
2. Separate scenario bullet lines, each prefixed by two em spaces (`U+2003`) and `• `. Bold the scenario label through its colon; keep the quoted follow-up in regular weight.
3. A regular-weight `Listen →` note prefixed by three em spaces, one indentation level deeper than the scenario bullets. Keep it visually secondary without italicizing the whole note or adding a bullet.

For example, the cell text should have this structure (apply the emphasis described above with native rich text):

```text
Confirm context: You’re evaluating the reporting workflow—have I got that right?

1. FOLLOW A REAL FINDING — “Could you walk us through one recent finding?”
  • If tickets drive the work: “Which system holds the ticket, and who owns each handoff?”
  • If evaluation happens elsewhere: “Where do the evidence and final decision live?”
   Listen → Fragmented records suggest testing traceability across the existing workflow.
```

Leave a blank line between main-question groups. Use literal em spaces, bullets, and native rich-text emphasis in the Sheet; do not insert Markdown markers or underscores as separators. The listening note is an operator cue for the group, not another question or a mandatory branch. Keep only the most useful branches and retain the stage-appropriate next-participant/action prompt in the final question group. Adapt topic titles and questions to the account’s evidence and current step; the example establishes layout, not a fixed discovery script.
