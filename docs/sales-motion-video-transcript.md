## Detailed reconstruction

### 00:00:00–00:08:53 — Framing the enterprise-sales lifecycle

The episode opens by promising a step-by-step walk through an enterprise sale, from earning the first meeting to completing the contract. The speakers immediately contrast this real customer journey with the familiar five-stage CRM forecast. CRM stages such as intro, demo, proposal, contracting, and closed are useful for probability-weighting a pipeline, but they are not a sufficient playbook for conducting the sale.

The central idea is that enterprise sales is a tightly managed, human buying process. A seller must understand the organization, keep the narrative specific, cultivate internal help, anticipate friction, and coordinate many small commitments. A generic playbook or an automated sequence cannot replace the judgment required.

### 00:08:54–00:18:42 — Step 1: Land the first meeting

The target is the executive decision-maker or the person immediately below them. When a founder and sales lead are both available, they can use a pincer approach: the founder reaches the executive while sales reaches the executive's direct report. The goal is not indiscriminate multithreading; it is coordinated access to the part of the organization that can understand and sponsor meaningful change.

The outreach should be extremely short. It should describe the strategic advantage—the episode calls this the “alpha”—rather than offering a generic efficiency claim or listing features. The message should help the executive see what they could accomplish, change, or become known for. Different channels may work for different markets, so email, LinkedIn, phone, events, and warm introductions should be treated as experiments whose results refine the message.

The broader lesson is that copied sales language rarely works without adapting it to the founder, product, buyer, and market. The message needs to feel materially different from the flood of ordinary enterprise outreach.

### 00:18:43–00:29:42 — Step 2: Run the introductory call

Jen describes the introductory conversation as the most important intelligence-gathering call in the process. It should be informal, buyer-led, and free from a slide deck or product demo. The seller should learn the organization's priorities, existing approach, desired change, decision criteria, internal dynamics, and timing.

The conversation should uncover why the issue matters now and what happens if the organization does nothing. It should also separate the value experienced by end users from the value understood by senior leadership. The strongest information is account-specific—facts a competitor would not know from public research or a standard qualification framework.

The seller should resist forcing the buyer through a canned sequence of qualification questions. The objective is to understand how this organization thinks and buys, not merely to fill CRM fields. If a senior executive attends, founder participation can increase credibility and signal the importance of the conversation.

### 00:29:43–00:38:20 — Step 3: Conduct the pre-demo follow-up

After the introductory call, the seller should hold a short follow-up with the emerging champion before the broader demo. This interaction is easy to skip, but the episode treats it as a major source of leverage.

The seller confirms what was heard, learns which parts of the product will resonate, determines who must attend, and asks what each participant cares about or may react poorly to. If one stakeholder needs a private preview before the group session, this is when to arrange it.

The champion should help shape the demo, including the questions the seller should ask and the way the session should be framed. That involvement makes the purchase feel internally authored rather than imposed by a vendor. The group demo should be booked while both sides are together instead of being left to an open-ended email thread.

### 00:38:21–00:46:44 — Steps 4 and 5: Prepare and run the group demo

Preparation begins with understanding every attendee: their role, priorities, influence, and likely concerns. The seller and champion should define what a successful session looks like, including the right mix of discussion and product. New stakeholders need a concise reset in the customer's language so the product immediately feels connected to their situation.

The group should include the people involved in deciding, using, sponsoring, and implementing the product. The opening should focus on the customer's current state and desired change, not the seller's corporate history. New participants should be invited to say what they want from the session.

The demonstration itself should stay narrow. Roughly 20 percent of the product often contains 80 percent of the relevant value, and the earlier conversations should reveal which 20 percent matters. Showing irrelevant capability can weaken the narrative, prompt objections about unused features, and make the price feel inflated. Questions can expand the demo naturally, but the core story should remain tight and account-specific.

The discussion also emphasizes that enterprise selling is closer to live project management and improvisation than to reciting a script. A strong salesperson should be able to explain why this demo is different, what will be shown, and how it will open.

### 00:46:45–00:48:58 — Step 6: Debrief immediately after the demo

As soon as the demo ends, the seller should contact the champion—ideally within minutes—and ask for an unfiltered reaction before the internal story solidifies. The seller wants to know where the session landed, where it lost people, who was excited or skeptical, and whether anyone needs a targeted follow-up.

The episode assumes that almost every complex deal has a person capable of blocking it. Early, candid feedback makes that risk visible. If the account later goes quiet, the accumulated stakeholder context lets the seller suggest a specific conversation rather than sending a generic follow-up.

The champion is defined functionally: the person who genuinely wants the initiative to happen and has helped shepherd it from early in the process. The champion may be an executive, but the role is not synonymous with title.

### 00:48:59–00:53:54 — Step 7: Define the pilot's path forward

Before opening a pilot, identify who owns it internally and which three or four people will actually use the product. The executive sponsor usually should not be treated as a daily user.

The seller should decide whether value can be demonstrated in a short sandbox without customer data or integration. If so, a tightly controlled two- or three-day experience can preserve momentum. If meaningful integration, configuration, or customization is necessary, a longer paid pilot may be appropriate; the fee can potentially be credited toward a final agreement.

The point is not free access. It is a bounded proof with a clear owner, real users, and an agreed commercial destination if it succeeds.

### 00:53:55–00:58:34 — Step 8: Prepare the pilot

Before users enter the product, both sides should define success precisely and select two or three concrete tasks. Individual or small-group onboarding can make the initial experience more reliable than one large session.

The seller should also work backward from the desired signature date. That requires mapping procurement, legal, security, IT, finance, and purchase approval before the pilot consumes the account's momentum. If the customer could not act for months even after a successful test, the pilot may be better delayed.

Pricing belongs after stakeholders have seen enough of the product to understand the value, often after the demo rather than after the pilot. The customer should know the likely commercial destination before investing time in evaluation.

### 00:58:35–01:04:41 — Step 9: Run the pilot

The pilot must be actively project-managed. Each participant should know exactly what to test, and the seller should monitor usage, completion, bugs, confusion, and variation between user experiences. When someone is inactive or stuck, the champion can help re-engage them.

Problems should be fixed while the pilot is running, not stored up for the final review. The seller needs concrete evidence of user and business value, plus enough product telemetry or direct feedback to understand what participants actually did.

The speakers again distinguish the real sales process from broad CRM buckets. A single “pilot” stage may contain preparation, onboarding, monitoring, intervention, evidence gathering, and decision planning. A successful process mirrors the buyer's real workflow instead of forcing the buyer into the seller's database structure.

### 01:04:42–01:11:30 — Step 10: Hold the post-pilot session

Feedback can be gathered through surveys, individual interviews, product data, the champion, or a combination. The seller should compare actual behavior with the agreed success criteria and ask directly what worked, what did not, and why.

The session should distinguish a real product gap from poor onboarding or low participation. Polite approval is not sufficient; the seller should surface objections and understand whether the group actually wants to proceed. A champion who suddenly stops responding is a meaningful negative signal.

The episode offers diagnostic benchmarks, not promises. It suggests a healthy enterprise win rate from qualified opportunity to contract is often roughly 25–35 percent, and that a well-qualified pilot should advance at a much higher rate—around 80 percent. A materially lower pilot conversion rate may indicate weak qualification, a flawed pilot design, or a product problem.

### 01:11:31–01:14:37 — Step 11: Prepare paperwork and the procurement timeline

The champion needs a concise email that can be forwarded internally, documenting pricing, scope, timing, commercial terms, and the reason for completing the agreement by a specific date. Urgency should be legitimate and connected to the deal, not manufactured pressure.

The seller should send an editable Microsoft Word agreement because procurement and legal are likely to redline it. The customer should be asked whether using the seller's paper or the customer's template will move faster.

Procurement should enter early enough to meet the target date. Its role is not inherently to kill the purchase; it is the function that makes the transaction conform to company policy and enables payment. Implementation should not begin before the agreement is complete.

### 01:14:38–01:15:54 — Step 12: Review the contract and redlines

Straightforward, low-impact edits should move quickly. Legal attention should focus on provisions that materially affect risk or economics. The seller should use qualified counsel and avoid treating every clause as equally important.

When redlines are extensive, prolonged email exchange is inefficient. Once the parties are largely aligned, a live legal conversation can isolate and resolve the remaining material issues.

### 01:15:55–01:16:42 — Step 13: Work through procurement and legal

The procurement and legal leads from both sides should discuss the terms neither party can accept. The seller may need to resist disproportionate insurance, liability, or other requirements by explaining the company's real constraints and exposure.

Customer paper is expected to be buyer-friendly, but many provisions remain negotiable. Procurement is a buying-process stakeholder whose incentives and requirements must be understood, not an automatic enemy.

### 01:16:43–01:17:55 — Step 14: Route and obtain the signature

Before routing the agreement, identify the person with actual signing authority. The executive sponsor, economic buyer, approver, and authorized signatory may be different people; the signer may be a CFO or another designated executive.

The champion should understand the routing path and expected date. If the agreement stalls, the seller can ask the champion to prompt the signer. The opportunity remains open until every required signature is complete.

### 01:17:56–01:23:30 — Practical step 15: Begin expansion planning

The conversation turns immediately from the signed agreement to expansion. For a mature product, the next teams or modules may already be obvious. For an early company and its first enterprise customers, founder involvement remains important because the initial deployment reveals what should become product, service, or a separate paid offering.

The speakers argue that services can be a legitimate part of an enterprise software relationship because customers already know how to buy them and often have meaningful service budgets. The first contract is therefore a landing point, not the limit of the opportunity.

The closing discussion returns to timing and buyer maturity. Some organizations are not ready to adopt the technology, and respectful timing can produce later “boomerang” deals. In other cases, the seller should use the buyer's own stated priorities to challenge a soft retreat. The judgment lies in distinguishing a solvable obstacle from a genuinely wrong time.

The final principles are to keep the process human, write messages in a real voice, preserve the account-specific intelligence gathered throughout the cycle, and continue managing the relationship after the initial win.

### 01:23:31–01:24:53 — Close

The hosts recap that the conversation produced a much more granular process than a traditional five-stage sales funnel. Jen points listeners toward enterprise-sales roles at State Affairs, and the episode closes with the standard podcast outro.
